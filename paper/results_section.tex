\section{Results}
\subsection{Numerical accuracy and finite-sample costs}
Table~\ref{tab:integrals} compares the four complete fitted-mean integrations with independent simulations. The largest discrepancy is \MaxIntegralZ{} Monte Carlo standard errors. In the $\rho=.95$ configuration, the upper clipping mass is approximately $.384$; retaining it is consequential.

Whitening, determinant and directional-variance identities agree to floating-point precision on the checked matrix grid. Five deterministic $m=2$ population-curvature checks have maximum relative discrepancy \CurvatureError\%. The numerical agreement is consistent with the identities derived in Appendix A.

The finite-calibration integral also reveals a numerical limit. At the most extreme tested finite rank, $m=2,n=9,k=9$, a 512-point Jacobi rule still differs from adaptive integration by \MaxJacobiError\%. The expectation is finite, but its inverse-CDF integrand is singular at one. Adaptive tail integration is therefore used for known-coefficient calibration means.

An additional fitting diagnostic evaluates $N\E(\widehat\rho-\rho)^2$ against $d\kappa/m$. At $N=500$ and $\kappa=1$ or 1.5, the estimates are near the limiting values. At $\kappa=3$, the discrepancies remain substantial: $.2174$ versus $.2700$ for $m=4$, and $.0756$ versus $.0900$ for $m=12$. The expansion is consequently unsuitable as an unchecked finite-sample equality under stronger scale heterogeneity. Figure~\ref{fig:costs} reports this diagnostic alongside calibration costs.

\subsection{Common Gaussian dynamics}
Table~\ref{tab:common} reports coverage and mean interval length for the eight comparison methods. IN-ARCP's mean-length ratios to the invariant Ad-EffOrt implementation are \AdaptFour{} and \AdaptTwelve{} at $m=4$ and 12. Its ratios to the same-fit Student reference are \StudentFour{} and \StudentTwelve{}, with paired Monte Carlo intervals containing one. The results therefore support efficiency relative to the particular invariant learned comparator while showing near parity with the classical reference.

Against native Ad-EffOrt, the corresponding ratios are \NativeFour{} and \NativeTwelve{}. The $m=4$ result favors the native procedure under unchanged amplitudes. The raw-RMS ablation is longer in both history settings, indicating a benefit from accounting for correlation in the history scale while holding the AR center fixed. Figure~\ref{fig:comparisons} displays their paired length ratios.

\subsection{Amplitude transfer and model misspecification}
Whole-episode doubling leaves coverage of the invariant constructions unchanged and doubles their widths, as expected from homogeneity. Native Ad-EffOrt and global AR calibration lose coverage in that arm (Table~\ref{tab:shift}). Future-only doubling reduces IN-ARCP coverage to \FutureFour{} and \FutureTwelve{}. The loss occurs when history and response no longer share the same episode multiplier, outside the premise of the coverage proposition.

Structural misspecification changes the relative efficiency. For independently varying episode coefficients, IN-ARCP has \VariableFour{} and \VariableTwelve{} times the mean length of the invariant nonlinear RF comparator at similar observed coverage. The direction of this result agrees with the limits of a shared linear center. In the heavy-tailed AR(1) setting, Student coverage is below the target, especially for $m=4$, while conformal calibration remains near the target. The supplementary tables report coverage and length for all methods and mechanisms.

\subsection{Training--calibration allocation}
The prespecified planning candidates reduce mean length relative to the equal $150/150$ split by \PlanOneGain\%{} for $\kappa=1$ and \PlanMixGain\%{} for $\kappa=1.5$ (Table~\ref{tab:planning}). The paired descriptive intervals lie below a ratio of one in these two evaluations.

The candidates lie near a broad group of efficient splits, rather than at a uniquely resolved empirical minimum. Some neighboring splits have slightly smaller sample means. Figure~\ref{fig:planning} displays every evaluated split and the discrepancy between the leading approximation and Monte Carlo means. Thus, the criterion identifies useful allocations in these settings, with small gains and limited separation among neighboring candidates. Its performance with estimated planning parameters remains to be evaluated.
